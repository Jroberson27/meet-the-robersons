# Meet The Robersons — Blog Voice Standard

Originally adapted from Orlando Informer's conversational tone, then corrected after reviewing roughly 50 real travel and theme park blog articles (family bloggers, Orlando Informer, Disney Tourist Blog, We3Travel, Laughing Lindsay, TouringPlans, WDWNT, and others). The first pass of this guide produced writing that read as AI generated. This version documents what real human travel writers actually do differently. Applies to all future blog posts on meettherobersons.com.

## Anti-patterns to avoid (this is what gave it away)
These constructions showed up nowhere in the ~50 real articles reviewed, but showed up constantly in the first draft of this voice. Cut them on sight.
- The scenario-then-reveal sentence: setting up a mini scene, then naming what it is, then generalizing about it. Example of what NOT to do: "Picture this: kids asleep in one room, you actually awake in another. That is the family suite at Cabana Bay, two queen beds plus a separate living area. That kind of separation is rare." Real writers just state it: "Our suite included two queen beds, a pull out sofa, a kitchenette with a sink, mini fridge, and microwave."
- Manufactured rhetorical questions used as a hook or transition, like "Want to level this room up even further?" or "Will you make it out of Hawkins?" Real writers ask a question only when they mean it literally, usually as a closer inviting comments, not as a structural device.
- "Here is the thing though," "Here is what mattered most to us though," "Picture this," and similar staged setup phrases. Just say the thing.
- Winking, self aware wordplay ("pun fully intended"). Real reviews in this space do not do this. Cut entirely.
- Grandstanding verdicts like "This is not just a good value hotel. For families, it might be the best hotel Universal has." Real bottom lines are short and utilitarian, see Verdicts below.
- Softening real complaints into "tradeoffs we will not pretend did not happen." Real writers state complaints flatly and specifically, sometimes with mild annoyance, and move on.

## What real travel writers actually do
- Plain declarative sentences. Subject, verb, object. Specifics get stated directly mid-sentence instead of being built up to: "$150 a night," "30 to 40 minutes," "430 square feet."
- Pros and Cons lists are a genuine genre convention, not a corporate crutch. Most real reviews break down into an actual Pros list and Cons list near the end rather than a single flowing "verdict" paragraph.
- Complaints are unglamorous and specific: a bandaid left in the bathroom, water pressure too weak for the shower, housekeeping never replacing a missing blanket. Small, plain, a little irritated. That specificity is what makes a review feel trustworthy.
- Verdicts are short. Example of the real pattern: "Bottom line: you aren't spending a lot of time in the room, and the pros (early admission, free shuttle, pools, kitchenette, extra space) outweigh the cons." One or two sentences, no superlatives.
- First person is direct and unfussy ("I," "we," "my kids"), not a tour guide voice like "we are walking you through."
- Opinions can be sharp and confident when a writer has one, stated once and plainly, not hedged with "might be" or "may be" language stacked on top of itself.

## The core register
Warm and direct, like a friend telling you what actually happened, not a brand narrating an experience. Emotional resonance can open a piece, but it should read like a genuine reaction, not a crafted "feeling before fact" formula applied to every section.

## Authority through casual specificity
Concrete, specific details dropped plainly do the credibility work that adjectives cannot: exact prices, exact minutes, exact square footage, specific small objects noticed in a room. Prefer a specific number or detail over a vague superlative every time.

## Headers stay short and keyword specific
Keep h2 section titles short and keyword driven: "The Family Suite," "Bayliner Diner: Food and Dining," "The Pools." The H1/page title can still be a hook style question (e.g. "Is This Universal's Best Value Hotel for Families?"), but h2 body headers should not be clever or promise style.

## Standing rules (unchanged, still apply on top of the voice standard)
- No dashes in copy. No em dashes or hyphens used as punctuation.
- Never reference family size or "family of 8."
- Blog h2 headings: `font-family: var(--font-body); font-weight: 900`.
- No visible image captions, alt text only.
- Blog buttons: `.blog-body a.btn { color: white; text-decoration: none; }`
- No word "honestly" in any copy.
